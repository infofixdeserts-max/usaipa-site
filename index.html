A field manual for the discarded half

Half of America has no voice.

Not because it is silent, but because it was never offered a sentence worth signing.

The Independent Parties of America is proposed as a new grouping of numerous independent parties, founded on the Constitution and the Bill of Rights. Its method is holistic management, adapted from the working landscape to the way people govern themselves. Its one requirement to run is not wealth or a famous name. It is authorship: you must have helped write your community's own statement of how it wants to live.

The method in one picture

Four moves, then a test that anyone can run.

A community writes the life it wants. Authors carry it upward. Every policy then faces the same seven questions, in the same order, in public.

1

Write the context

The community answers three questions in writing: how people want their lives to be, what must be produced to live that way, and what the land, water and soil must look like centuries from now.

Consensus, not compromise
2

Elect the author

Only people who helped write the context may run on the IPA ticket. The community votes, and its representative carries that written word to the next table up: city, county, state, nation.

Authorship is the price of entry
3

Test every policy

Seven honest questions, asked in order, judge every policy a candidate writes or votes on. Most proposals fail at question one, because they treat a symptom while the cause keeps its lobby.

Public property, any voter can run them
4

Fail safe

Every policy carries a fail-safe clause written in advance. The moment an unintended consequence is confirmed, the policy is void and goes back to public debate from scratch.

Harm is expected, not excused
Did the policy pass all seven?
NoIt dies in the open, before it costs anyone a decade.
YesIt stands with its fail-safe clause attached and its reasoning on the record.

The seven questions

Hard to fool, easy to check.

They come from four decades of decisions about land, money, animals and human communities. They are not a philosophy test. They are an order of operations.

1

Cause and effect

Does this action address the root cause of the problem?

2

The weak link

Does it strengthen the weakest link in the chain toward the community's quality of life?

3

Marginal reaction

Of the time and money spent, which choice returns the most on the next dollar and the next hour?

4

Gross profit

Which enterprise goes furthest toward covering the overhead the community carries?

5

Energy and money

Where do the energy and the money come from, where do they go, and is the power addictive in use?

6

Sustainability

Does it move toward the future resource base the community wrote down?

7

Society and culture

How do you feel about it now, and will it harm the lives of people outside the community?

Any policy, any voter

Why a third foundation

Roughly half of eligible voters skip most elections.

They are not lazy. They have been offered the same two choices for decades, and neither one asked them to author anything. A ballot with nothing of yours on it is a ballot you can skip without guilt.

Where it starts

One county seat, not one capital.

The first races are local, winnable and visible: school board, county commission, small city mayor. One officeholder whose published record shows the seven questions used in office is worth more than a thousand national arguments online.
