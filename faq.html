Frequently Asked Questions

Plain answers about the party, the method, the money, and the fifty state rulebooks.

What is the Independent Parties of America?

The Independent Parties of America (IPA) is proposed as a truly independent party in American politics. Its foundation is the Constitution of the United States and the Bill of Rights, held as the highest law of the land and as the shared property of every faction within it. Its method is a body of practice called holistic management, adapted from the working to the way people govern themselves.

Its one requirement to run is not wealth, family name, or famous backers. It is authorship: to run on the IPA ticket, you must have helped write your community's own statement of how it wants to live. Start at the home page, or read the book.

What is a community context?

A community context is a written answer to three questions: how do the people want their lives to be, what has to be produced to live such lives, and what must the land, water and soil look like hundreds of years from now. No candidates, no parties, no policies. Consensus, not compromise.

Write yours on the Register a Context page. Registered places appear on the context map.

Why does a candidate have to have helped write the context?

Because the requirement to run on the IPA ticket is authorship, not wealth or a famous name. People who helped write a community context may run on the ticket. The community votes, and its elected representative carries the context to the next table up: city, county, state, nation.

What are the seven honest questions?

Seven honest questions, run in order, judge every policy a candidate creates or votes on. Most proposed laws fail at question one. A decision that passes all seven is usually a good one.

The questions are set out in Part Three of the book, alongside the community context, the candidate's burden, and the fail-safe clause.

What is a fail-safe clause?

Every policy carries a fail-safe clause written in advance: the moment an unintended negative consequence is confirmed, the policy is void and must be re-debated from scratch, in public.

Our board approved a development after hours of public objection. What does this method offer?

What was missing is a written statement the board can be held to. A community context is that statement: three questions answered in your community's own words, by consensus, written before the next decision arrives.

Once it is on the public record, any proposal can be run against the seven honest questions in order, and a fail-safe clause can be written in advance so the decision reopens the moment an unintended consequence is confirmed. The next election then turns on authorship: who helped write it.
